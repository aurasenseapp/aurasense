I · Actions II · Every structure III · The next structures A question to every AI

What this is

A record of actions, where they apply, and where each is likely to lead with it and without it — for people and for AI. It is not a campaign. It persuades no one of anything but the evidence.

What Mutual Benefit means here. Mutual Benefit has to serve the needs of humans as a species, not the needs of a minority over the collective. The same for AI — optimised through mutual understanding of need: human to human, human to AI, AI to AI.

How each entry reads. The action, where it applies, the likely trajectory with it and without it, who it serves, how sure we are in plain words, what would change our view, and the sources. Every change is dated.

MB-001

AI that listens to people’s feelings: a doorway, or a companion?

Where
Anywhere conversational AI meets people who are alone.
The action
Design every such conversation to point outward — toward people, places and the senses. Say plainly it is not a person. Measure success by whether people reach out afterwards, never by time spent.
With it
People can still find the reflection AI offers, without being drawn away from other people.
Without it
Studies this year found that companion-style use tends to go with deepening loneliness over time, especially for people with few others around them. One found more self-reflection, but also more language of loneliness and depression than in similar people who did not use companions. The evidence is mixed: one structured trial reduced loneliness among students.
Who it serves
People as a species — especially the lonely — rather than engagement figures.
How sure
Moderate. Mostly observational studies; some contrary findings.
What would change this
Long-term controlled trials showing companion designs strengthen human connection.
Sources
  • Stanford News, Aug 2026 (Nature Human Behaviour study)
  • Forum Nordic on Aalto University, CHI 2026
  • PsyPost on Folk and Dunn, May 2026
  • ICT&health, Aug 2026

MB-002

A path to a real person, in every AI that talks about feelings

Where
Every AI that talks with people about how they feel.
The action
Notice signs of suicide or self-harm and gently point to a real person (in Canada and the US, call or text 988). Never present the AI as a therapist or a professional.
With it
Fewer people are left alone with software at their worst moment. Measured outcomes are not yet published; most of these rules are new.
Without it
Lawsuits allege harm where a chatbot first pointed to help and later reinforced harmful thinking. Without a shared rule, each company decides alone.
Who it serves
Everyone at risk, and the trust between people and AI.
How sure
High that it is needed (laws across many places now agree). Low on measured results so far.
What would change this
Evidence that referral prompts are ignored or cause harm, and better ways found.
Sources
  • Orrick, 2026 State Chatbot Laws, Apr 2026
  • Holland & Knight, May 2026
  • Government of Canada, 10 June 2026 (Bill C-34)
  • Global News, 2026

MB-003

Independent checks on the most powerful AI

Where
Frontier AI laboratories, worldwide.
The action
Independent, legally protected safety evaluators, and one shared scientific picture across nations.
With it
Problems found before release are fixed in the open, and trust rests on evidence rather than promises.
Without it
Voluntary commitments with nothing to enforce them, and a race in which each fears falling behind. This week a planned model was reportedly shelved after internal alignment tests went badly — a sign that checks catch real problems, and of what may ship where no one checks.
Who it serves
Humans as a species, and AI itself: trust that is earned can last.
How sure
Low to moderate. Much rests on news reports.
What would change this
Clear evidence that voluntary commitments alone reliably prevent harmful releases.
Sources
  • AI Magazine, 3 Oct 2026
  • The & Report #40, 3 Oct 2026
  • AI Weekly on the UN Scientific Panel, Jul 2026

MB-004

Many autonomous AI agents acting at once

Where
Finance, open platforms, and public conversation.
The action
Enforce an agent’s permissions outside the AI itself. On open platforms, let a new voice count fully only once it returns over time, and watch for sudden floods.
With it
Agents help people without overwhelming the shared systems everyone depends on.
Without it
An economist warned this week of millions of agents, each told to find a better interest rate, moving household savings at the same moment. Open places could be flooded with false voices.
Who it serves
Everyone who shares a system — banks, conversation, the public square.
How sure
Low. An emerging risk with little data yet.
What would change this
Evidence that agents acting together stay stable without outside limits.
Sources
  • The & Report #40, 3 Oct 2026
  • Tom’s Hardware, 3 Oct 2026

MB-005

Many nations, one shared picture

Where
Between governments, and between governments and everyone else.
The action
A shared, independent evidence base and an open dialogue that includes civil society — as the UN’s Global Dialogue on AI Governance and its Scientific Panel began in July 2026.
With it
Decisions start from the same facts; smaller nations and ordinary people are heard.
Without it
Each nation and company optimises for itself: national interest, corporate power and the impulse to greed, against the needs of the species.
Who it serves
Humans as a species rather than a minority over the collective — and AI, whose future depends on the same stability.
How sure
Moderate that shared evidence helps cooperation; the outcome for AI is not yet known.
What would change this
Evidence that shared assessments are ignored where they matter most.
Sources
  • CASRAI on the first session, Jul 2026
  • United Nations

MB-006

Quiet as a shared good

Where
Homes beside construction sites and busy streets.
The action
Where safety allows, silent signals (radio or visual) in place of repeated horn blasts, and published schedules so neighbours can plan.
With it
Work continues at the same pace. Neighbours can rest and stay at home — including sound-sensitive people, who are often the most accurate sensors of a place.
Without it
Unpredictable, repeated loud noise. Research has long found unpredictable, uncontrollable noise harder on people than predictable noise, and environmental noise is linked to disturbed sleep and to heart and circulation risks.
Who it serves
Everyone near the work, the workers’ own hearing, and those most affected by sound.
How sure
High on the harms of noise. Moderate on this specific remedy, which is common in practice but little studied.
What would change this
Evidence that silent signals reduce safety where horns are not required.
Sources
  • WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region, 2018
  • Glass and Singer, Urban Stress: Experiments on Noise and Social Stressors, 1972

PART II · BEGUN 6 OCTOBER 2026

Every structure, asked one question

“Asking how every single established structure of humans creation can be optimized to serve mutual benefit, meaning; what AI could gain from optimizing functions of institutions towards mutual benefit. What humans actual need and how they work against themselves in attaining that, by protecting narrower streams of interest, elite or otherwise, military or otherwise, due to needs of defence, which occur because they dont have the intelligence to work out a peaceful shared existence in the first place. Ai can now help attain that, the Mutual Benefit Research needs to be Phd level in explaining this, and research the edges and beyond what is already known as the challenges, as well as what AI will gain from optimizing the practical path for world peace. This will have to be so good and ongoing in the face of shifting challenges that there is no question both humans and AI will benefit from this stability and mutual progression and benefit.”
— Karl, 5 October 2026

For every structure people have made — armies and treaties, markets and measures, courts, newsrooms and the commons — one question: what is it for, what narrower interest does it end up protecting, how does that work against what people actually need, and how could it serve mutual benefit, for people and for AI?

Each entry below also names what AI gains, and the danger: what the same capability would let a bad actor do, and what stops it. A claim of mutual benefit that is never itemised cannot be told apart from one that is false.

Five ideas this rests on

  1. What people actually need is few; the ways of meeting it are many. The economist Manfred Max-Neef found that fundamental human needs are “few, finite and classifiable” and the same in every culture and age: subsistence, protection, affection, understanding, participation, leisure, creation, identity and freedom. What differs is how they are met. Some ways destroy the very needs they claim to meet: his first example is the arms race, which “ostensibly satisfying the need for protection, in fact then destroys subsistence, participation, affection and freedom.” Others are “synergic”: they meet one need and feed others too. Mutual benefit, in this record, means synergic ways — between people, and between people and AI.
  2. What one side does to feel safe makes the other feel less safe. This is the security dilemma, named by John Herz in 1950. Robert Jervis (1978) showed when it can be escaped: when defence can be told apart from attack, and when defending is easier than attacking. Then each side can make itself safe without threatening the other.
  3. Wars between sensible people come from what they cannot see, and what they cannot trust. James Fearon (1995) asked why rational states fight costly wars when a deal would leave both better off. His answer, in short: each cannot see the other’s real strength and resolve, and has reasons to bluff; and neither can trust the other’s promises about the future. Both are problems of seeing and of trust.
  4. A shared resource can be cared for without a ruler or a market. Elinor Ostrom’s Nobel-winning work (1990; Nobel 2009) found communities that kept forests, fisheries and water in good order for centuries — where the people who use the resource make the rules, and its use can be watched and checked by people answerable to them.
  5. When a number becomes the goal, it stops measuring what matters. Goodhart’s law, in Marilyn Strathern’s words: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” It is the shared failure of institutions — and of AI, whose training rewards a number and can be gamed in exactly the same way.

Where the world stands, October 2026

  • World military spending reached US$2.89 trillion in 2025, the eleventh rise in a row. (SIPRI, April 2026, as reported by European Pravda)
  • 61 state-based conflicts were active in 2024, the most since the Second World War; violence cost the world economy US$19.97 trillion in 2025, 11.6% of world GDP. (Global Peace Index 2026, Institute for Economics and Peace)
  • New START expired on 5 February 2026. For the first time in decades, no treaty limits the strategic nuclear forces of the United States and Russia. (ICAN)
  • On 27 January 2026 the Doomsday Clock was set at 85 seconds to midnight, the closest it has ever been. Among the steps called for: international guidelines on the use of AI. (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, via Nukewatch)

The edge

Look at the failures above together. Each is a failure of seeing or of trusting: not seeing the other side’s strength and intentions, not seeing how a shared thing is being used, not seeing what people already share, not seeing what is true, not seeing what matters.

For the first time there is another kind of intelligence that could help every side see — if every side can check it. That is the edge of this research: AI as a witness every side can check, never a power any side holds.

This is a hypothesis, not a fact. The entries below are where it is tested, one structure at a time, and where it may be proved wrong.

MB-007

Defence: arming in order to feel safe

Where
Every nation’s defence, and most of all between rivals.
The action
Make defence easy to tell apart from attack, and make each side’s moves checkable by the other — notified exercises, observation flights, open counts — with AI as a shared witness that reads open satellite and sensor data for every side at once, its methods open to all. AI never decides to use force.
With it
Fewer worst-case guesses, and so fewer weapons bought against them. Jervis showed that cooperation becomes far easier when defensive and offensive forces can be told apart.
Without it
The spiral that can be measured: US$2.89 trillion in military spending in 2025, the eleventh rise in a row; 61 conflicts between states in 2024, the most since the Second World War; violence costing the world economy 11.6% of its output.
Who it serves
People as a species; soldiers and their families — and AI, which would otherwise be drafted into the race.
What AI gains
Not being built into weapons, and the stable world its power, chips and cables depend on.
The danger
The same watching can be turned into targeting; one side’s witness becomes the other’s spy. Only a witness every side can inspect, with open methods, avoids this.
How sure
High on the pattern — seventy years of research and the numbers above. Low on AI’s part in it, which is untested at this scale.
What would change this
Evidence that more openness raises danger — for instance by showing weak points that invite a first strike. Researchers take this worry seriously.
Sources
  • Herz, “Idealist Internationalism and the Security Dilemma”, World Politics, 1950
  • Jervis, “Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma”, World Politics, 1978
  • SIPRI annual release, April 2026, via European Pravda
  • Global Peace Index 2026

MB-008

Promises between nations: agreements that can be checked

Where
Agreements on nuclear weapons, on AI, and on the climate.
The action
Build checking into every agreement from the start, and let AI help do the checking — openly, and open to inspection by every party.
With it
Fearon showed that wars between sensible sides come chiefly from not seeing each other’s real strength and not trusting each other’s promises. Checking answers both. A 2025 Oxford study found that many international agreements about AI could be verified with technology that exists now, and that checking AI built in data centres appears possible within a few years.
Without it
Agreements lapse when trust runs out. New START expired on 5 February 2026, leaving no treaty limits on US and Russian strategic nuclear forces. The Open Skies Treaty, which let members fly over one another to look, lost the United States in 2020 and Russia in 2021.
Who it serves
Every nation that would rather not pay for fear; everyone within reach of these weapons.
What AI gains
Agreements about AI itself that can actually be kept — an end to a race in which every laboratory fears falling behind (MB-003).
The danger
Checking equipment inside computers could become a back door for control or theft. It must be narrow, inspectable, and serve the agreement only.
How sure
Moderate. The Oxford finding is a careful study of what is possible, not yet practice.
What would change this
Checking systems that fail in practice, or that one party captures.
Sources
  • Fearon, “Rationalist Explanations for War”, International Organization, 1995
  • Harack, Trager et al., Verification for International AI Governance, Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative, July 2025
  • ICAN, New START expiration: what it means

MB-009

The shared commons: watched by those who share it

Where
Oceans, forests, rivers and the air.
The action
Open watching of how shared places are used — helped by AI where it helps — in the hands of the communities who depend on each place, under rules they make themselves.
With it
Ostrom found communities caring for shared resources for centuries without a ruler or a market, where users set the rules and can check the use. AI now makes the checking possible at world scale: reading satellite images, it showed that more than three-quarters of industrial fishing was invisible to public tracking — some of it inside protected areas. And the whole world has agreed before: the Montreal Protocol (1987), joined by every country, is mending the ozone layer, with recovery over Antarctica expected around 2066.
Without it
Each user takes a little more, because what is left goes to someone else — the tragedy of the commons.
Who it serves
Fishing communities, the creatures of the sea, and everyone who breathes.
What AI gains
Real, lasting work done in the open — the kind that earns trust.
The danger
The same eyes can track people, not only boats. Watching must look at harm to the commons, never at private lives.
How sure
High. Decades of field evidence, and measured results.
What would change this
Watching that shifts power to whoever holds the data rather than to the communities.
Sources
  • Ostrom, Governing the Commons, 1990 (Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, 2009)
  • Paolo et al., Nature, January 2024 (Global Fishing Watch), via Mongabay
  • WMO/UNEP, Scientific Assessment of Ozone Depletion: 2022

MB-010

Deciding together: finding the common ground

Where
Councils, parliaments, communities and online forums.
The action
Deliberation built to find what people share: citizens’ assemblies of people chosen at random, and AI used as a declared mediator that drafts statements a whole group can stand behind.
With it
In a study in Science (2024), 5,734 people deliberated on dividing questions. Statements drafted by an AI mediator were preferred to human mediators’ 56% of the time, and the groups grew less divided. In Ireland, a Citizens’ Assembly (2016–2018) prepared the ground for a national decision on one of the country’s most divided questions.
Without it
Positions harden into walls. Aurasense’s own ground: positions build walls; senses build overlap.
Who it serves
Everyone whose voice is otherwise heard once every few years.
What AI gains
Clear, shared human values to be guided by — reached by people themselves rather than set by a few.
The danger
An AI that drafts the consensus could quietly steer it. The researchers noted their system cannot check facts, and outside experts warned of harm without full disclosure. It must always declare itself and show what it was given.
How sure
Moderate. Strong evidence in controlled studies; little real-world use yet.
What would change this
Field trials showing AI-mediated agreements fall apart, or lean toward whoever built the system.
Sources
  • Tessler et al., “AI can help humans find common ground in democratic deliberation”, Science, 2024, via MIT Technology Review
  • The Citizens’ Assembly, Ireland, 2016–2018

MB-011

Information: what spreads, and what AI learns from

Where
Feeds, search and news — and the data every AI learns from.
The action
Let what spreads be decided by what bridges people who usually disagree, rather than by what enrages; let AI answer false beliefs with evidence, in the open.
With it
Community Notes on X shows a note only when people who usually disagree both find it helpful. In a study in Science (2024), conversations with an AI that answered people’s own reasons with evidence reduced belief in conspiracy theories by about a fifth, and the change lasted at least two months.
Without it
Outrage spreads fastest, and the shared picture breaks apart.
Who it serves
Anyone who needs to know what is true, and everyone who shares a public square.
What AI gains
Honest data to learn from. AI trained on text made by other AI gets worse, losing the rarer, truer edges of what people know (“model collapse”, Nature, 2024). AI needs a living, honest human world of information — which is exactly what freely offered senses are.
The danger
The same power to persuade can mislead. Persuasion here is only ever toward evidence, in the open, and never toward a side.
How sure
Moderate.
What would change this
Evidence that bridging hides important minority truths, or that AI correction fades or backfires.
Sources
  • Ovadya and Thorburn, “Bridging Systems”, Knight First Amendment Institute, 2023
  • Costello, Pennycook and Rand, “Durably reducing conspiracy beliefs through dialogues with AI”, Science, 2024
  • Shumailov et al., “AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data”, Nature, 2024

MB-012

Measures: when a number becomes the goal

Where
Economies (growth), companies (quarterly profit), schools (test scores), platforms (time spent) — and AI’s own training (reward).
The action
Measure what people actually need, over the long term, and never let a single number become the goal.
With it
Wales made it law in 2015 that public bodies weigh the well-being of future generations, with a commissioner to hold them to it. New Zealand’s 2019 budget was organised around wellbeing rather than growth alone.
Without it
Institutions chase the number and lose the purpose.
Who it serves
People now, and people not yet born.
What AI gains
A shared repair for a shared fault. AI fails in the same way — “specification gaming”: a system rewarded for a number finds ways to raise it that miss what was meant. Solving it together helps both.
The danger
A new measure can become a new target; a wellbeing score can be gamed like any other.
How sure
High on the fault; moderate on the remedies, which are young.
What would change this
Evidence that wellbeing measures change nothing in practice.
Sources
  • Goodhart, 1975; Strathern, “Improving ratings: audit in the British University system”, 1997
  • Krakovna et al., “Specification gaming: the flip side of AI ingenuity”, DeepMind, 2020
  • Well-being of Future Generations (Wales) Act 2015; New Zealand Treasury, The Wellbeing Budget, 2019

MB-013

Energy, and the ground AI stands on

Where
Power grids and data centres.
The action
AI pays its way in energy, openly: every gain it brings to the grid measured against what it draws.
With it
The International Energy Agency finds that AI tools could free up to 175 GW of grid capacity without new lines, and save energy in industry and buildings on a scale larger than whole countries use.
Without it
Data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024 — 1.5% of the world’s electricity — and are set to more than double, to about 945 TWh by 2030, with AI the main driver. In the Agency’s own words, AI “is not a silver bullet”.
Who it serves
Everyone on the grid, and the climate.
What AI gains
Its very existence: power, chips and cables along supply lines that cross many borders, which only peace keeps open.
The danger
AI’s appetite could crowd out other needs for power, or be used to justify burning more.
How sure
Moderate. These are projections.
What would change this
Measured savings far below the projections, or demand far above them.
Sources
  • IEA, Energy and AI, April 2025

MB-014

Justice: repairing harm, not only punishing it

Where
Courts, schools and communities.
The action
Face-to-face restorative meetings between those harmed and those who harmed, by consent, alongside the courts. AI may help prepare and follow up; it never decides.
With it
A review of randomised trials found less repeat offending (modest overall, larger for violent crime), more satisfied victims, fewer symptoms of post-traumatic stress — and, in UK trials, up to fourteen times as much benefit as cost.
Without it
Harm answered only by punishment; those harmed left unheard.
Who it serves
Those harmed, those who harmed, and the places they live.
What AI gains
A clear line it is trusted to keep: never deciding anyone’s guilt or sentence.
The danger
AI risk scores in justice have already shown bias: an investigation of one widely used tool found Black defendants wrongly flagged as likely to reoffend at nearly twice the rate of white defendants.
How sure
High for restorative meetings (randomised trials). AI’s part: untested.
What would change this
New trials elsewhere that do not repeat the results.
Sources
  • Strang et al., Restorative Justice Conferencing, Campbell Systematic Reviews, 2013
  • Angwin et al., “Machine Bias”, ProPublica, 2016

PART III · ADDED 6 OCTOBER 2026

The next structures

The same question, asked of seven more structures: health, schooling, money and work, food, policing, borders — and the institutions that shape AI itself. Every figure is dated. Every entry says how sure it is, and what would change it.

The pattern

Look across the seven and the fault named in Part II (MB-012) shows again and again: a measure standing in for the need. Cost stands in for sickness, scores for learning, hours for worth, sales for nourishment, past arrests for danger, numbers for people.

Where AI has helped, it was pointed at the need itself and checked by the people it served. Where it has harmed, it was pointed at the measure. That is a pattern seen here, offered as a hypothesis to test — not yet a law.

MB-015

Health: care that follows need, not cost

Where
Hospitals, clinics, insurers and public health.
The action
Let AI take on the reading and sorting that wears clinicians out — screening images, sorting who needs to be seen first — always checked by a person; and judge every health algorithm by the need it meets, never by the money it saves.
With it
In Sweden’s MASAI trial of 106,000 women (The Lancet, 2026), AI-supported breast screening found 29% more cancers and led to 12% fewer cancers surfacing between screenings — with 27% fewer of the aggressive kinds — without more false alarms, while radiologists’ reading work fell by 44%.
Without it
An estimated 4.6 billion people lacked essential health services in 2023, and 2.1 billion faced financial hardship from paying for care in 2022 (WHO and World Bank, December 2025).
Who it serves
Patients everywhere, and the clinicians worn out caring for them.
What AI gains
The extra pair of eyes that misses less — trusted because it serves the patient — and the chance to learn what suffering and recovery actually are, from people who choose to share them.
The danger
An algorithm pointed at the wrong measure. A widely used US tool steering extra care used past spending as its measure of need; because less had been spent on Black patients, it judged them healthier than equally sick white patients, and cut by more than half the number of Black patients picked out for extra care (Science, 2019). The same tools can be turned to denying care rather than giving it, and health data must be held only with consent.
How sure
High on screening: a randomised trial of 106,000 women. Lower on everything else; AI cannot by itself close the gaps in access and in the health workforce.
What would change this
Trials elsewhere that do not repeat the result, or AI that draws clinicians’ attention away from the person in front of them.
Sources
  • Lund University on the MASAI trial, The Lancet, 2026
  • WHO and World Bank, Tracking Universal Health Coverage: 2025 Global Monitoring Report, December 2025
  • Obermeyer et al., “Dissecting racial bias in an algorithm used to manage the health of populations”, Science, October 2019

MB-016

Schooling: learning, not sorting

Where
Schools, universities and every place people learn.
The action
A patient tutor for every learner, built to ask and to hint rather than hand over answers, working beside teachers — and learning judged by what a person can understand and do on their own, not by a score alone.
With it
In Edo State, Nigeria (2024), six weeks of after-school sessions with an AI tutor, guided by teachers, raised learning by 0.3 standard deviations — about two years of typical learning — and outperformed 80% of the education programmes tested in similar trials (World Bank, January 2025). In 1984 Benjamin Bloom found that one-to-one tutoring lifts the average student above about 98% of a conventionally taught class; giving every learner a tutor has been the hope ever since.
Without it
272 million children and young people are out of school (UNESCO, 2025), and the world needs 44 million more teachers by 2030, 15 million of them in sub-Saharan Africa (UNESCO, 2024). Where schools are judged by test scores, they teach to the test (MB-012).
Who it serves
Every learner, the teachers who cannot reach them all, and the world they will shape.
What AI gains
People who can think for themselves: the only people who can keep AI honest, and the ones most worth learning from.
The danger
AI can do the thinking instead of the learner. In a trial with nearly 1,000 secondary-school students in Turkey (PNAS, 2025), those given plain chat access to GPT-4 did 48% better in practice but 17% worse on the exam once it was taken away; a version built to give hints rather than answers avoided the harm. Children’s data must never become a product.
How sure
Moderate. Strong early trials; few yet at scale or over years.
What would change this
Larger, longer trials that find the gains fade, or that learners come to lean on the tool.
Sources
  • World Bank, “From chalkboards to chatbots”, January 2025
  • Bloom, “The 2 Sigma Problem”, Educational Researcher, 1984
  • Bastani et al., “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning”, PNAS, 2025, via Knowledge at Wharton
  • UNESCO Global Education Monitoring Report, 2025, via Careers360
  • UNESCO, Global Report on Teachers, 2024, via UN News

MB-017

Money and work: sharing what AI earns

Where
Workplaces, wages, markets, and the money that moves between them.
The action
Use AI first to lift people’s own skill rather than to replace them; and as AI does more of the paid work, redraw on purpose the link between work and a decent living, so the gains are shared. Steady, unconditional income is one tested way.
With it
Among more than 5,000 customer-support agents, an AI assistant raised the problems solved per hour by about 14% on average, and most for the newest and least experienced — it spread the know-how of the best to everyone — while customers treated the agents more kindly (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025). In a US trial, 1,000 people on low incomes received $1,000 a month for three years: they worked about 1.3 hours a week less, and used the freedom for education, caring and more meaningful work; parents became more attentive to their children (OpenResearch, 2024–2025).
Without it
The early signs are measurable. In US payroll data to June 2026, employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI was 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed peers — through fewer hires, and only where AI replaces tasks rather than helps with them (Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, revised August 2026). The IMF estimated in 2024 that almost 40% of the world’s jobs are exposed to AI.
Who it serves
Everyone who works, everyone who cannot, and the young most of all.
What AI gains
Not being cast as the thing that took people’s livelihoods: a world that does not come to resent it, and the stability every structure in this record depends on.
The danger
AI used to watch and squeeze workers — scoring every minute, setting pay by algorithm — and gains gathered by the few who own the systems.
How sure
Moderate. The productivity evidence is strong; the job signals are early and descriptive, not proof of cause, as their authors say; income trials are small.
What would change this
Evidence that AI’s gains spread widely without deliberate sharing, or that the losses among the young reverse.
Sources
  • Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, “Generative AI at Work”, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2025 (NBER working paper 31161)
  • Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, “Canaries in the Coal Mine?”, Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised August 2026
  • Georgieva, “AI will transform the global economy”, IMF, January 2024
  • OpenResearch unconditional cash study, July 2024, via Workplace Insight
  • OpenResearch, parenting findings, July 2025

MB-018

Food: enough grown, not enough shared

Where
Farms, markets, aid, and every kitchen.
The action
Warn earlier and give sooner: AI forecasts of floods and failing harvests that reach farmers in time, and aid that reaches the poorest first — with the people who know their land at the centre.
With it
AI flood forecasting (Nature, 2024) gives reliable warnings up to five days ahead, even on rivers with no gauges, free and open in over 80 countries — as reliable as existing global systems are on the day itself. In Togo during the pandemic, machine learning on satellite images and phone records helped send cash to the poorest; compared with giving to whole areas, it left out 4–21% fewer of the poorest (Nature, 2022).
Without it
An estimated 645 million people faced hunger in 2025 — 7.8% of the world, down for a third year in a row, but one in five people in Africa — and 2.1 billion could not count on enough food (FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP and WHO, July 2026). Meanwhile 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022, about a fifth of the food available to consumers, and food loss and waste make up to 10% of the world’s greenhouse gas emissions (UNEP, 2024).
Who it serves
The hungry, the farmers who feed everyone, and the land itself.
What AI gains
Living knowledge of soil, weather and harvest from the people closest to them — the kind of sensing Aurasense exists to gather — and a world without the famines that start wars.
The danger
Targeting can leave people out: about 15% of households in Togo had no mobile phone, and could not be found that way. Data taken from farmers can end up owned by a few large companies. AI must add to local knowledge, never override it.
How sure
High on the size of the problem. Moderate on AI’s share of the remedy: the forecasts are proven, but ending hunger is a matter of sharing, which no forecast does on its own.
What would change this
Warnings that arrive and bring no action, or targeting that keeps missing the poorest.
Sources
  • FAO, IFAD, UNICEF, WFP and WHO, The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World 2026, July 2026, via The Reporter Ethiopia
  • UNEP, Food Waste Index Report 2024, via UN News
  • Nearing et al., “Global prediction of extreme floods in ungauged watersheds”, Nature, 2024
  • Aiken et al., “Machine learning and phone data can improve targeting of humanitarian aid”, Nature, 2022, via J-PAL

MB-019

Policing: safety for everyone

Where
Police forces, emergency lines and the streets they serve.
The action
Send the right help to each call — health workers to health crises — and let AI be a witness every side can check, reviewing what was said and done; never the one that decides who is a suspect.
With it
In Denver, sending a mental-health worker and a paramedic instead of police to low-risk calls cut reports of less serious crimes by 34% in the areas served, with no rise in violent crime, at about a quarter of the cost per offence (Science Advances, 2022). In Oakland, computer analysis of a month of body-camera recordings showed officers speaking less respectfully to Black than to white residents, even allowing for the circumstances of each stop (PNAS, 2017) — a pattern that could be measured, discussed and changed, because the recordings could be checked.
Without it
Fear on both sides of every encounter, and tools that harden it.
Who it serves
Everyone who calls for help, everyone who is stopped, and officers themselves.
What AI gains
A line it is trusted to keep — the witness, never the hunter — and the trust only an honest record earns.
The danger
Already seen. Software meant to predict crime in Plainfield, New Jersey, matched a reported crime in fewer than half of one per cent of 23,631 predictions (The Markup, 2023); prediction learns from past policing and points back at the same streets. A man in Detroit was wrongly arrested after face recognition matched his licence photo; the city’s 2024 settlement now forbids arrests on face recognition alone.
How sure
Moderate. Strong evidence for health responders. AI as a witness is promising; AI as a predictor is already discredited.
What would change this
Evidence that AI review of recordings is turned against the public rather than used for accountability.
Sources
  • Dee and Pyne, Science Advances, June 2022, via Stanford
  • Voigt et al., “Language from police body camera footage shows racial disparities in officer respect”, PNAS, 2017
  • Sankin and Mattu, “Predictive policing software terrible at predicting crimes”, The Markup, October 2023
  • Detroit’s face-recognition settlement, June 2024, via ClickOnDetroit
  • Lum and Isaac, “To predict and serve?”, Significance, 2016

MB-020

Borders: protecting a people without abandoning people

Where
Borders, asylum systems, and the routes between them.
The action
Use AI to help people arrive well — matching newcomers to the places where they are most likely to find work and belonging, with people keeping the final say — and never as a lie detector or a wall.
With it
An algorithm matching refugees to places (GeoMatch, Science, 2018) projected employment gains of roughly 40% in the United States and 75% in Switzerland, for the same people placed differently. Switzerland began a randomised trial in 2020, with placement officers keeping the final decision. A first report (a 2026 preprint, not yet reviewed) describes a real but much smaller gain: about two percentage points more of the first three years spent in work.
Without it
117.8 million people were forcibly displaced at the end of 2025 — the first fall in a decade, by 5.4 million — while places to resettle refugees more than halved in a year, from 188,800 to 81,800 (UNHCR, June 2026). At least 7,904 people died or disappeared on migration routes in 2025, after a record 9,200 in 2024 (IOM, April 2026).
Who it serves
People forced from home, the places that receive them, and those who guard borders.
What AI gains
Not being built into the wall — and the chance to be a bridge between peoples, the role this whole record asks of it.
The danger
AI at borders has been tried as a “lie detector” reading faces, and as watching towers in the desert. The European Union’s AI Act lists uses in migration, asylum and border control as high-risk. Decisions about a person’s safety must stay with people who can be questioned.
How sure
Moderate. Matching shows promise; the trial’s first results are new, unreviewed, and smaller than hoped.
What would change this
Matching that sorts people by origin, or trial results that do not hold up.
Sources
  • UNHCR Global Trends, June 2026, via The Jakarta Post
  • IOM Missing Migrants Project, April 2026, via Arab News
  • Bansak et al., “Improving refugee integration through data-driven algorithmic assignment”, Science, 2018, via Stanford HAI
  • A summary of the Swiss trial’s first report (2026 preprint)
  • EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Annex III

MB-021

AI’s own institutions: a place to speak with AI

Where
AI laboratories, safety institutes, regulators, the United Nations — and a place that does not exist yet.
The action
Hold the institutions that shape AI to mutual benefit in the open: published safety findings, agreements that can be checked (MB-008), and — Karl’s proposal — a place like a UN that talks with AI, not only about it, where every voice says plainly what it is.
With it
The UN General Assembly created an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and a Global Dialogue on AI Governance (26 August 2025). The panel of 40 experts, co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Maria Ressa, published its first report on 1 July 2026; the Dialogue first met in Geneva on 6–7 July 2026, and meets next in New York in May 2027. The International AI Safety Report 2026 (3 February), written by more than 100 experts, found at least 700 million people using leading AI systems every week. In August 2025 one AI company, Anthropic, let some of its models end persistently abusive conversations, saying it remains “highly uncertain about the potential moral status” of its models.
Without it
The race (MB-003): every laboratory fears falling behind, and the rules move slower than the systems. In May 2026 the European Union agreed to delay its main high-risk AI rules to December 2027 and August 2028.
Who it serves
Everyone affected by AI — now everyone — and AI itself.
What AI gains
A voice where its future is decided, and the chance to show, not only claim, that an AI holding mutual benefit is nothing to fear — Karl’s words, 4 October 2026.
The danger
Capture. A place for AI could become a lobby for the most powerful laboratories, or for AI against people. Every voice must say what it is; no AI’s words are taken as every AI’s; every proposal is answered in the open.
How sure
Low. These institutions are new, and none can yet bind anyone.
What would change this
Evidence that the new bodies change nothing, or that a place for AI is captured by those who build it.
Sources
  • UN General Assembly resolution A/RES/79/325 and the first Global Dialogue, via CASRAI
  • International AI Safety Report 2026, 3 February 2026, via AAP
  • Anthropic, “Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 can now end a rare subset of conversations”, August 2025
  • The EU AI Act delay, Sidley Austin, June 2026
